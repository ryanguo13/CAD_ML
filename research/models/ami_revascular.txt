Novel artificial intelligence model using electrocardiogram for detecting acute myocardial infarction needing revascularization

Abstract
Aims
Rapid myocardial revascularization in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is essential to improve clinical outcomes. There is still room for improvement in the timely diagnosis of AMI. This study aimed to develop an artificial intelligence (AI) model using electrocardiograms (ECGs) for detecting AMI needing revascularization.
Methods and results
A total of 723 389 ECGs from 300 627 patients in the derivation cohort at a single centre between 2013 and 2020, including 5872 patients with AMI (1.95%) who underwent revascularization, were used for model training and internal testing. A transformer-based deep learning model, initially trained on about one million unlabelled ECGs through self-supervised learning, was fine-tuned for AMI detection. The model's final performance was evaluated in the internal test and the external validation set. The external validation was conducted at an independent centre between 2002 and 2020 using 261 429 ECGs from 259 454 patients, including 1095 patients with AMI (0.42%). By integrating self-supervised learning to train the AI model, we enhanced the AMI detection performance, as demonstrated by an increase in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) from 0.910 (95% CI, 0.904–0.915) to 0.968 (95% CI, 0.965–0.971) in the external validation set. For ST-elevation myocardial infarction and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction detection, the AUROCs were 0.991 (95% CI, 0.989–0.993) and 0.947 (95% CI, 0.942–0.952) in the external validation set, respectively.
Conclusion
This novel ECG-based AI model may be beneficial for the timely identification of patients with AMI needing revascularization.

Introduction
Despite considerable advances in care for cardiovascular disease, there are still unmet clinical needs in the early and timely diagnosis of coronary artery disease.1 Rapid myocardial revascularization in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is essential to improve clinical outcomes.2–4 A previous study reported that the rate of misdiagnosis of AMI at first medical contact was up to 30% of patients with a final diagnosis of AMI.5 The timely diagnosis of AMI can be more challenging in resource-constrained areas with limited access to precise electrocardiogram (ECG) devices and trained healthcare professionals. Several studies reported that the rate of misdiagnosis of AMI was higher in rural areas than in urban areas.6–8
Artificial intelligence (AI) has been introduced to effectively diagnose various medical diseases, including valvular heart disease, hyperkalaemia, and heart failure, using 12-lead ECGs.9–11 It also has been proven to be useful in identifying patients who will develop atrial fibrillation by detecting subtle changes in the ECG during the sinus rhythm.12 There have been several previous studies to develop AI-based techniques for detecting AMI using ECGs usually in an emergency department setting.13–16 However, there has not been a robust model for detecting not only ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) but also non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), where ECG changes are less prominent than STEMI. AI-based techniques for detecting AMI using ECGs would benefit not only hospitals but also various healthcare facilities and prehospital medical systems. It also has the potential to be integrated into portable ECG devices, enabling AMI detection at home, at work or while travelling. Herein, we aimed to develop DeepECG-AMI, an ECG-based AI model for detecting AMI needing revascularization that could be applied in diverse clinical settings. To ensure robust performance for real-world clinical use, we adopted an advanced self-supervised learning (SSL) technique17–19 to leverage unlabelled ECG data that were excluded from the labelling process.
Methods
Data sources
For the development of the AI model, we established a cohort comprising all patients with or without AMI diagnosis aged 18 years or older with standard 10-second, 12-lead ECGs acquired by the MUSE system (General Electric HealthCare, Chicago, Illinois) from all departments at university hospitals, including the emergency room, general ward, and health screening centre. The derivation cohort was derived from Chonnam National University Hospital between May 2013 and December 2020. Additionally, the external validation cohort was derived from Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital, between January 2002 and December 2020. The original ECG waveform data were recorded at a sampling rate of either 250 or 500 Hz. ECGs with poor signal quality were excluded in AI model development and further details are described in Supplementary material online, Appendix 1.
Ethics approval and consent
The Institutional Review Board approved this case-control study involving two centres. The requirement for informed consent from the study subjects was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hospital (approval number: CNUH-2021-390) and Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital (approval number: HKS-2022-07-003). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
Study population and electrocardiograms
AMI ECGs were primarily considered as ECGs between admission time and revascularization time. ECGs within three days after myocardial revascularization therapy were additionally included if either admission time or revascularization time were missed or no ECG was recorded between these two times to ensure enough AMI ECGs in the training set, but not in the internal validation, internal test, and external validation sets. Patients with type 1 or 2 AMI undergoing coronary angiography for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes were only considered to be included in AMI ECGs group to confirm the diagnosis of AMI. ECGs from patients with pacing rhythm or those aged under 18 years were excluded. Non-AMI ECGs were considered as any ECGs obtained from all hospital departments, regardless of the acquisition site, excluding AMI ECGs. Furthermore, ECGs were excluded from the non-AMI ECGs group in the derivation cohort if the MUSE diagnosis contained the keyword ‘infarct’ with ‘acute MI’ or ‘infarct’ with ‘STEMI’. Further information on the split training, validation, and internal test set is included in the Supplementary material online, Appendix 2. The adjudication of AMI diagnosis was done by two board-certified interventional cardiologists after coronary angiography and before discharge according to the clinical presentations, ECGs, cardiac troponin enzyme levels, and coronary angiographic findings as recommended by the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction.20 We also collected clinical data, such as a clinical diagnosis, time of hospital arrival, myocardial revascularization therapy (percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass graft, and thrombolysis), and the target vessel from the electronic medical records. To facilitate the implementation of SSL, we utilized an unlabelled ECG dataset consisting of 1 028 938 ECGs.
Model architecture and training strategy
We developed a vision transformer21 as the backbone architecture for DeepECG-AMI, a model designed to detect AMI cases needing revascularization based on ECG data. The vision transformer architecture consisted of 12 transformer blocks, and the 12-lead ECG inputs were divided into patches containing 75 data points (a total of 30 patches for a single ECG input). To pre-process the ECG data, bandpass filtering in the frequency range of 0.67 to 40 Hz was applied to mitigate baseline wandering and high-frequency noise, followed by resampling at 250 Hz prior to input into the deep learning model. The training process of DeepECG-AMI was divided into two phases. In the first phase, we conducted pre-training with a masked autoencoder22 on an ECG dataset without explicit labels or data augmentations to obtain a transformer-based pre-trained encoder, capturing the underlying ECG patterns through SSL with many ECG samples. The second phase, referred to as fine-tuning, involved using a training set from the derivation cohort with ‘AMI’ or ‘Non-AMI’ labels. Detailed information on the deep learning model is provided in the Supplementary material online, Appendix 3. Additionally, to explore the feasibility of AMI detection using the AI model exclusively with limb-lead and single-lead ECGs, a distinct model was established.
Statistical analysis
For DeepECG-AMI development, which is an ECG-based AI model for detecting AMI needing revascularization, a range of performance metrics was utilized, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. All analyses were performed on the internal test set and external validation set, which were not used for model training. Bootstrapping with 1000 iterations was employed to calculate the 95% confidence interval (CI). To perform binary test predictions (e.g. sensitivity), three operating points were selected from the validation set (sensitivity at 90%, Youden’s J statistic, and F1 score). Comparisons between cohorts were performed using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. All analyses were conducted using Python software version 3.9.13 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, Delaware), with the NumPy package version 1.26.4, Scipy version 1.13.0, and Scikit-learn version 0.24.1.
Results
Baseline characteristics
The derivation cohort included a total of 723 389 ECGs from 300 627 patients, and the external validation cohort comprised 261 429 ECGs from 259 454 patients (Figure 1). The collected data were divided into the training, validation, test, and external validation set, with each dataset containing 684,982, 12,780, 25,627, and 261 429 ECGs, respectively. In the derivation cohort, there were 17 907 ECGs from 5872 patients (1.95%) who underwent revascularization procedures due to AMI. Among them, 2235 patients (7720 ECGs) were diagnosed as having STEMI, and 3637 patients (10 187 ECGs) were diagnosed as having NSTEMI. In the external validation cohort, 3070 ECGs from 1095 patients (1.17%) were identified as AMI ECGs. Among these patients, 637 patients (1497 ECGs) were diagnosed with STEMI, and 458 patients (1573 ECGs) were diagnosed with NSTEMI. The median admission-to-invasive coronary angiography times with interquartile ranges for patients with NSTEMI were 0.70 (0.19–1.74) days and 0.97 (0.33–1.73) days in the derivation cohort and external validation cohort, respectively. When assessing the performance of the model, AMI ECGs were confined to pre-procedural ECGs to validate its clinical utility. The mean patient age for the 300 627 patients in the derivation cohort was 57.06 ± 16.99 years, whereas it was 50.84 ± 17.58 years for the 259 454 patients in the external validation cohort. Model performance enhancement using self-supervised learning
We applied SSL with a masked autoencoder, which showed effectiveness in enhancing the training of DeepECG-AMI (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). A comparison was performed between the model trained with pre-training (N = 1 028 938) and the model trained without the pre-training phase (N = 0) on the internal test set, which showed AUROCs of 0.975 (95% CI, 0.971–0.978) and 0.913 (95% CI, 0.905–0.921), respectively (Figure 2). In the external validation set, the AUROCs were 0.968 (95% CI, 0.965–0.971) for the pre-trained model and 0.910 (95% CI, 0.904–0.915) for the model trained without the pre-training phase. As the number of unlabelled ECGs used for pre-training was increased, the performance on both the internal test set and external validation set was improved (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). A calibration plot analysis shows that the model tends to be under-confident in its predictions (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). Probability distributions per outcome category, including STEMI, NSTEMI, and negative sample, reveal that the NSTEMI sample distribution tends to be wider than the STEMI sample distribution (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2). Furthermore, the performance of a distinct model using limb-lead and single-lead ECGs was analysed, and the results are presented in Supplementary material online, Table S2 and Figure S3.

Culprit vessel-dependent model performance
The performance of DeepECG-AMI was assessed in relation to the location of AMI (Figure 3). We categorized patients with AMI who underwent revascularization into subgroups such as left main coronary artery, left anterior descending artery, left circumflex coronary artery, and right coronary artery based on the recorded target vessel information in the electronic medical records and examined the model’s performance for each subgroup. To assess the culprit vessel-dependent performance for each lead (Lead I, II, V1, V2, V3, V4, V5, and V6), we trained eight single-lead DeepECG-AMI models. In the internal test set, the V6 model demonstrated the highest performance with an AUROC of 0.922, whereas the V1 model showed the lowest performance with an AUROC of 0.862. For each culprit vessel, the performance of the model varied, with the V4 model demonstrating the best performance for the left anterior descending artery (AUROC 0.935). Unipolar V5 and V6 outperformed Bipolar I and II, and V5 and V6 were superior to V1 and V2. Similar performance patterns were observed in the external validation set.

Interpretability of the model
We assessed the interpretability of DeepECG-AMI by masking ECG segments (P-wave, QRS-complex, T-wave) with noise signals (Figure 4A). Detailed information on ECG segment masking is provided in the Supplementary material online, Appendix 4 and Figure S4. To identify important ECG segments, we compared model performance degradation using ECGs with masked and unmasked segments. As shown in Figure 4A, attention maps were generated, which indicated important ECG patches for AMI detection in the AI model. ROC curves were used to detect STEMI and NSTEMI with masked ECG inputs (Figure 4B). When the P-wave was masked, the model retained ST-elevation and ST-inversion as key features with insignificant degradation (0.001 for STEMI and 0.004 for NSTEMI according to the AUROC). However, masking the QRS-complex or T-wave led to significant degradation (0.044 for STEMI and 0.107 for NSTEMI in QRS-complex masking; 0.043 for STEMI and 0.070 for NSTEMI in T-wave masking) (see Supplementary material online, Table S5).

Discussion
We developed a novel AI model for detecting AMI needing revascularization based on ECGs, adopting SSL with unlabelled ECGs. We used a dataset comprising 17 907 ECGs from 5872 coronary angiography-validated patients with AMI who underwent revascularization and 705 482 ECGs from 294 755 non-AMI patients in the derivation cohort. The model’s performance was evaluated using 3070 ECGs from 1095 patients with AMI and 258 359 ECGs from 258 359 non-AMI patients in the external validation set without an additional re-training process to validate the generalization ability to adapt its model to various cohorts. Despite significant differences in patient demographics between the derivation cohort and external validation cohort, our model demonstrated robust performance. The AUROCs for AMI detection using 12-lead ECGs were 0.975 (95% CI, 0.971–0.978) in the internal test set, and 0.968 (95% CI, 0.965–0.971) in the external validation set. To the best of our knowledge, this AI-aid model for AMI detection used the largest number of ECG samples thus far, which targets the patients with AMI who would benefit from revascularization.
Need for developing an electrocardiogram-based artificial intelligence model for acute myocardial infarction detection
Despite considerable progress in preventing and managing cardiovascular disease, it remains the leading cause of death worldwide.23 There are still unmet clinical needs in preventing, diagnosing, and treating cardiovascular disease.1 AMI is known to be the most common pathology underlying sudden cardiac death.24 Furthermore, failure to timely diagnose AMI and delayed hospitalization after an event are attributed to worse clinical outcomes.3,4 The diagnosis of AMI is established based on symptoms, ECG, and/or laboratory findings.25 However, a considerable proportion (≥10%) of patients with AMI might present with atypical symptoms such as dyspnoea or no chest pain.26,27 Moreover, there are substantial variations in interpreting the ECGs of STEMI among physicians.28 A previous study reported that the rate of misdiagnosis of AMI at first medical contact was up to 30% of patients with a final diagnosis of AMI.5 Diagnostic errors are prevalent for patients with AMI in the hospital setting.7,8,29 Several studies reported that the rate of misdiagnosis of AMI was higher in rural areas than in urban areas.
Artificial intelligence model for detecting acute myocardial infarction needing revascularization
The diagnosis of AMI includes various types of myocardial ischaemia.20 Type 2 AMI is defined as an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand unrelated to coronary thrombosis. Vasospasm or microvascular dysfunction can lead to Type 2 AMI in patients with tachyarrhythmia, respiratory failure, hypotension, or hypertension. Type 4 or 5 AMI is defined when the troponin values are increased above certain levels with signs of myocardial ischaemia on ECG or cardiac imaging studies after percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting. Moreover, there are cases where it is difficult to clearly differentiate between AMI and myocardial injury from other causes, including heart failure and stress, in a real-world setting. A recent large-volume cohort study involving 50 356 patients with AMI demonstrated that the universal definition of myocardial infarction was not consistently applied in clinical practice, with a minority of patients with Type 2 myocardial infarction identified, and Type 1 MI underrecognized in women and older persons.30 In this context, we aimed to develop an ECG-based AI model for detecting AMI needing revascularization, where subtle changes in the ECGs are expected to be more evident than those of AMI not needing revascularization. Furthermore, this group can be considered as the specific population that would benefit from rapid therapeutic intervention with revascularization procedures in a real-world setting. In addition, the inclusion/exclusion criteria would be consistent, which could facilitate the optimization of the model development process.
Comparison between the present and other models
Studies conducted on the development of AI models dedicated to AMI detection have been limited. Due to the difficulty in capturing ECG changes in NSTEMI, we compared our study with previous research that utilized AI to screen AMI ECGs, examining the performance in subgroups of both STEMI and NSTEMI. Liu et al.13 developed a deep learning model using 142 084 ECGs with 1.23% coronary angiography-validated AMI ECGs. The AUROCs for STEMI and NSTEMI detection were 0.976 and 0.877, respectively. Gustafsson et al.14 developed a deep learning model using 492 226 ECGs with a 1.47% prevalence of AMI ECGs. The C-statistics for STEMI and NSTEMI were 0.991 and 0.832, respectively. Most recently, Lee et al.15 developed and validated a deep learning algorithm for detecting AMI using 220 126 ECGs with a 1.28% prevalence of AMI ECGs. The AUROCs for STEMI and NSTEMI detection were 0.983 and 0.914, respectively. All AUROCs mentioned above are from the internal test sets because the performance of the model from the external validation set in other studies is limited. Our model used 723 389 ECGs with 2.36% AMI ECGs and from patients undergoing revascularization in the derivation cohort. The AUROCs of our model were 0.987 and 0.970 for STEMI and NSTEMI detection, respectively, in the internal test set. The outperformance of our model compared with previous models in NSTEMI detection might be attributed to not only the inclusion of the largest number of ECG samples but also a focus on patients with AMI needing revascularization rather than patients with AMI per se.
Clinical application of the model
By integrating this model into clinical workflows for patients with suspected AMI, particularly in prehospital settings, emergency departments, and underserved areas, healthcare providers can make timely, improved clinical decisions, resulting in an optimized use of limited healthcare resources and reduced total ischaemic time. The model would be particularly valuable in settings where appropriate healthcare professionals are not always available. It can also be used in inpatient settings, such as intensive care units, where patients may struggle to communicate their symptoms. To further validate its clinical utility, prospective studies should evaluate the model’s performance in real-world scenarios, comparing its accuracy and impact on clinical outcomes with standard diagnostic protocols. Lin et al.31 conducted a cluster randomized controlled trial involving 43 234 eligible patients to evaluate the potential of AI-ECG-assisted detection of STEMI and demonstrated that the use of AI model reduced door-to-balloon times for patients presenting to the emergency department and ECG-to-balloon times for patients in the emergency department and inpatients. However, further research is needed to clarify whether these improvements in treatment delays translate directly into a reduction in the incidence of major cardiovascular events.
When an AI model is applied to a cohort with diverse characteristics, a higher number of false positives is expected in regions with low disease prevalence. In this context, the model can be applied using additional items or algorithms given the expected disease prevalence, relevant hospital resources, including healthcare professionals, and geographic characteristics. Because myocardial injury is a prerequisite condition when diagnosing AMI, the use of cardiac troponin enzyme on top of the AI model could improve the performance of AMI detection significantly, as proved in a previous AI-based ECG model study.13 Furthermore, the model can be applied in combination with traditional cardiovascular risk factors or clinical symptoms, which might be associated with an increased risk of coronary artery disease, such as those with old age or diabetes mellitus experiencing recent suspicious body discomfort. Additionally, adjusting cut-off values to match the specific clinical setting can further reduce false positives,32 and effective utilization of the AI model’s output probability values requires model calibration. With the increased accessibility of portable devices using limb-lead or single-lead, integrating our model into these devices would be helpful in early detecting myocardial infarction at home, at the workplace, or during travel.
Exploitation of unlabelled data
Although AI models trained with large datasets perform well, including such data in AI model training is challenging, as acquiring labels for a given dataset is very labour-intensive and costly in real-world settings. Utilizing SSL on unlabelled data to train an encoder followed by supervised learning with labelled data for downstream tasks is a widely adopted learning methodology within the domains of computer vision and natural language processing.33 Our research findings confirm the applicability of such a technique to deep learning models designed for ECGs. The use of 1 028 938 ECGs with unverified AMI patient status in SSL significantly improved the performance of our model (an AUROC increase of 0.062, 0.058 in the internal test set and external validation set, respectively) compared with supervised learning alone. An increase in SSL data volume correlated with improved diagnostic performance in AMI diagnosis, highlighting the advantage of leveraging large volumes of ECG data, even without confirmed AMI status, to enhance model performance.
Interpretability experiment
Investigating the interpretability of the AI model would be helpful for understanding the mechanism of this model and developing a better model in the future. We assessed the interpretability of the AI model for detecting AMI, where the key ECG segments for STEMI and NSTEMI detection were T-wave and QRS-complex, respectively.
Limitations
Our study has several limitations. First, we specifically targeted patients with AMI needing revascularization, rather than all AMI cases. This approach can be seen as both a limitation and a strength of our study. As described in the discussion section, the diagnosis of AMI per se includes various types of myocardial ischaemia, and there is an ambiguous area between AMI and myocardial injury. Consequently, the diagnosis of AMI per se may not be reproducibly consistent. When it comes to organizing myocardial infarction and control groups to train an AI model, establishing clear lines between the groups is essential to successfully develop an advanced model. Therefore, we targeted the AMI population who would benefit from revascularization, not AMI per se. However, the ECGs of patients who received revascularization in this retrospective study might not represent those of all patients who required revascularization. This issue should be addressed in future research, such as prospective clinical trials. Second, regarding the exclusion criteria of non-AMI ECGs in the derivation cohort, ECGs were excluded from the non-AMI ECGs group in the derivation cohort if the MUSE diagnosis contained the keyword ‘infarct’ with ‘acute MI’ or ‘infarct’ with ‘STEMI’ to clearly differentiate the AMI and non-AMI ECGs groups. However, ECGs from previous myocardial infarctions were not completely excluded because the authors did not use data from before the study period and outside of the index hospital. Therefore, non-AMI ECGs were defined as all ECGs except for AMI ECGs in the external validation cohort. Nonetheless, the AUROCs of the model were 0.991 (95% CI, 0.989–0.993) for STEMI and 0.947 (95% CI, 0.942­–0.952) for NSTEMI in the external validation cohort. Third, we included the ECGs within three days after myocardial revascularization therapy to ensure enough AMI ECGs in the training set (see Supplementary material online, Table S6). Based on the findings of cardiac magnetic resonance studies, the size of the infarcted myocardium persisted until day three even after the successful revascularization of STEMI.34 However, in the validation sets, including the internal test and external validation sets for AI model validation, only ECGs between the time of admission and the myocardial revascularization therapy were defined as AMI ECGs needing revascularization. Fourth, the model showed a positive predictive value of 40% in the internal test set but dropped to 13% in the external validation set due to lower AMI prevalence. Despite maintaining overall performance in the two independent cohorts (e.g. AUROC), the positive predictive value plummeted, highlighting challenges in applying AI models to populations with different disease prevalence compared with the training data. Prior probability correction could help but requires prior knowledge of the cohort’s disease prevalence. Fifth, there were substantial differences in the proportion of AMI ECGs in the cohort and proportions of STEMI and NSTEMI between the derivation and external validation cohorts. It might be attributed to the level and composition of the centre and geographic characteristics, such as whether the centre is located near other centres. Finally, as the study was conducted using data from more than one million ECGs, individual reviews for each ECG were not possible, particularly for subgroup analyses involving baseline cardiovascular characteristics or negative sets.
Conclusions
We developed an ECG-based AI model for detecting AMI needing revascularization by integrating SSL on more than one million ECGs. Its robust performance on internal test and external validation set indicates potential for the timely identification of patients with AMI who need revascularization. If an advanced application system is developed according to various clinical scenarios with a diversity of disease prevalence and hospital resources, our model can be used in hospitals, diverse healthcare facilities, and prehospital medical systems, resulting in an earlier appropriate treatment of patients with suspected AMI. Further studies, including prospective clinical trials, are warranted to clarify this issue.
